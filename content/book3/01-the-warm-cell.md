# Chapter 1
## The Warm Cell

The latch on the inner door stuck, as it always stuck, and Cardinal Solenne put her shoulder to it without calling for a novice. Heat spilled over her knuckles. The Warm Cell was a square of dressed basalt no wider than a confession stall, lit by a brazier the size of a begging bowl, and it had been her true office since the night she laid Halvern under glass. She did not sit. She never sat when the work was waiting. She took the iron spiral in the floor and went down into the Vault of Cinders with one hand on the rail, counting the turns the way other women counted beads.

The vault received her the way a held breath receives a name. Six coffins stood in a ring around a shallow dish of cinder-glass, each coffin the length of a grown body and the color of a quenched forge, each bound with seals that had once been bright as well-shards and were now the dull red of old scabs. The air down here was not the thin rationed warmth of Cindrel's streets. It was a stored heat, a hoarded heat, the leftover fever of six people who had tried to be the sun and had been put to bed for it. Solenne walked the ring as she always walked it, clockwise, and she spoke the names because unnamed things learned how to get up.

Vael first. The Kiln. The coffin was pitted along one side where his heat had tried to eat the iron from within, four hundred years ago, before the Church had understood that a vessel could starve a city by shining. She set two fingers on the lid and felt the slow pull of him, the old hunger that had never learned to be a hearth. Torren next, the captain who had warmed an army until the army cooked in its own armor; his seals were military knots, ugly and sure. Iven after that, who had written that the Hollow Sun was a door and not a corpse, and who had opened himself to prove it. Iven's glass was the clearest. She could see the outline of a man with his hands folded, and if she was not careful she could mistake the pose for peace.

Halvern's coffin stood at the fourth mark, and she stopped there longer than the rite required. The glass over him had been poured in a single night with her own calorancy in the mix, and it had never clouded. He looked as he had looked at twenty-nine, which was a cruelty she had chosen and would choose again. Dark hair. A mouth that had argued with her in kitchens and in choir lofts and once, disastrously, in the well-court in front of the old Cardinal. She had loved that mouth. She had put a seal over it anyway. *Managed dusk*, she had told him, when he still had a voice to call her a coward. *A noon that belongs to one body will eat the rest.* He had not believed her. The Hollow Sun had twitched in its socket the night he peaked, the same way it had twitched for the boy in Vireth, and she had done the only work left to a person who loved the world more than one man. "I kept the cell," she said, which was not a prayer. "It is still too warm. You would complain."

Sera's coffin was smaller. That was the fact of it, and Solenne did not decorate the fact. Twelve years old, wrists like kindling, a well-brat from the ash-lanes who had lit a whole district by accident and then asked, politely, if she could light the rest. Inquisitor Voss had held the child still while the seals went on. Voss had been twenty and certain. Solenne had been certain too. Certainty was a tool. She used it until it broke, and then she had it reforged. The glass over Sera showed a girl with her eyes closed and a smear of gold still caught in the lashes, as if noon had tried to leave a forwarding address.

The sixth had no name because names were how vessels taught the living to miss them. He had been a slum boy from the canal-side of Cindrel, fourteen or fifteen, caught in the first hour of his waking with a three-legged mutt barking itself hoarse at his heels. He looked enough like the Vireth boy that Solenne had, for one unprofessional breath, wondered if the god recycled faces. She had sealed him before he learned a technique worth copying. His coffin hummed at a pitch the others did not, a thin, unfinished note. She had always disliked that note. It sounded like a sentence that had been cut off in a kitchen.

The dish at the center of the ring was new work. She had spent the last year on it, in the hours when the court wanted hymns and Cassian Vale wanted to be told he was a sun. The dish was reverse-calorancy: six channels of seal-iron inlaid in a pattern Iven had once sketched in a margin and then crossed out, because Iven had still believed in doors. The pattern did not open. It drank. If the six were chained through it, their stored noon would pull inward instead of spilling, a snuffing well, a throat for heat. A weapon against a seventh. She had not told the novices what it was. She had told them it was a reliquary. In her experience the two were often the same object. She crouched and checked the inlay with a thumbnail. One channel had hairline frost in it, which was wrong. Frost did not belong in the Vault of Cinders. Frost belonged to the woman in the Nightlands who called herself a mother and marched like a season. Solenne scraped the frost out with the edge of a seal-key and watched it die on her skin as a single bead of water. *Not yet*, she thought, and was not sure whether she meant the frost or the weapon.

A sound came down the spiral: boots, then the particular cough of a man who had been told not to run in holy places and had run anyway. Brother Kest appeared on the last turn with his cap in his hands and ice in his eyebrows. He was young enough to still believe that ice on a messenger was a kind of proof. "Eminence," he said, and then, because she did not fill silences for other people, he filled this one himself. "The north gate sent a flare. Frost-line is inside the outer farms. And the cistern watch reports heat that is not on the tithe-books. Gold heat. Moving."

Solenne stood. Her knees did not crack. She had spent too much stored warmth on the small vanities of a body that still had work to do. "Cassian?" Kest's mouth did something unhappy. "In his apartments, Eminence. He asked not to be called unless the disk moved. He has not worn the saint-harness since the boy beat him without killing him. The shard in his chest is quiet. The hymn-keys are still in his keeping." She did not raise her voice. Kest leaned in to hear her, which was the old trick, and she watched him understand that he had been given an order dressed as a forecast. "They will not be by evening. Go back up. Seal the well-court. No one in or out of the Spire but Church. If Mother Ash wants a conversation, she may have it with the walls."

Kest hesitated. The hesitation was the most honest thing he had brought down the stairs. "The gold heat, Eminence. The watch thinks it is him. The Vireth boy. In the cisterns. Inside the city." Solenne said, "I know what they think." She knew because the sixth coffin had changed its note while Kest was still talking. The unfinished hum had found a harmony. It was a small sound, smaller than a kettle, and it made the hair along her arms lift as if a door had opened in a house she had locked herself. The other five lids did not move. Halvern did not move. But the ring as a whole had the attention of a room that had been waiting for a seventh chair.

Solenne crossed to the nameless coffin and laid her palm on the glass. The boy inside did not wake. He was not allowed to. What woke was the leftover calor in him, the scrap of god-heat that recognized its own kin walking the pipes above their heads. Ren Kael. Street rat. Primordial Calor with a dog and a pair of borrowed adults. The dream becoming lucid, if you believed the old Nightland sermons. A furnace, if you believed Vael. A child, if you were tired and made mistakes. She did not make that mistake.

"The seventh is inside the city," she said, for the vault, for Halvern, for the dish that would drink him. "We will not chase him through alleys. We will let him come down. He has a habit of running toward dying things." She looked at Sera's small lid and then away from it, which was as close as she came to flinching. "When he does, we chain the six. We snuff him. We keep the dusk we have." Kest swallowed and asked about the frost-queen at the gates, and Solenne told him the truth because the vault was a poor place for comfort. "Then we have two problems, and I have always preferred two problems to a noon."

She turned from the ring and started up the spiral, because the Warm Cell would need a second brazier tonight and the court would need a lie about relics, and because standing over Halvern any longer would turn into a conversation she could not afford. At the third turn she paused and spoke down into the stored heat without looking back. "Stay asleep. All of you. I am still doing this for you." The sixth coffin's note followed her up the stairs like a child at a market stall. She shut it out with the latch, which stuck, as it always stuck. In the Warm Cell the brazier burned at the temperature of a sleeping man, and the black disk of the Hollow Sun, glimpsed through the slit window, hung over Cindrel as it had hung for a thousand years: lightless, watching, and, she was almost certain, no longer entirely asleep.
