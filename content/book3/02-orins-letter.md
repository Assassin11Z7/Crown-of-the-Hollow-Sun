# Chapter 2
## Orin's Letter

Ren went through the grate shoulder-first and came down in water that had no right to be this cold inside a capital. Cinder landed after him on three legs and shook once, insulted, then put his nose into the dark as if the dark owed him rent. Lyra dropped last, sabers already clear of the coat, the frost-flower crown making a small dry sound against the inner lining like someone tapping a warning. The cistern tunnel ran north under Cindrel in brick the color of old tea. Somewhere above them the Church was counting heat. Somewhere ahead, if Orin's last scrap of chalk had not been a joke, a boy who had publicly sold them was waiting with a lamp he was not supposed to have.

"If he brought a squad, I get the first punch," Lyra said. Ren told her that if Orin had brought a squad, Cinder got the first punch and she could have the second, because he was a generous god. "You are a wet god," she said, and went past him because she still walked like an inquisitor when the ceiling was low.

They moved. Ren kept the Primordial Calor down in the floor of himself the way you kept a coal in a closed fist: present, expensive, stupid to open. Every time he used it the black disk over the world took a slightly more interested breath. He had learned that at the pass, in thirty heartbeats of noon, and he had not stopped learning it since. The cisterns helped. Stone drank leaks. He let a thread of heat-sense run along the mortar, a trick he had copied off a well-guarded in Thornveil and then made quieter, and the thread found a second warmth that was not Lyra and not the dog. Glassheart warmth. Orin's. It sat in a side chamber like a man trying not to be a beacon and failing because he had never been built for dim.

Orin Hale was nineteen and looked like he had not slept in a currency the court accepted. He had a Church coat on, too fine for the cistern, and a bruise along his jaw that had nothing to do with training. When Cinder went to him, tail doing the whole traitorous dance, Orin went down on one knee in the water and put his face in the dog's neck as if that were a legal way to say sorry. Ren stood there with his hands empty and his mouth full of every joke he had practiced and none of them fit. He had let Orin go on the glass steps of the court. He had watched the perfect lie land. It had hurt then. It hurt now in a different register, like coming into a room and finding the fire still lit.

"You look like a tax," Ren said. Orin laughed once, which was a cracked sound, and stood. He asked whether they had followed the chalk marks or the heat, and Ren said the dog had followed him and he had followed the dog and Lyra had followed the part where this was a terrible idea. "Nice collar. Very betray-your-friends. Gold thread. I would have gone with something in 'please kick me.'" Orin said it was the smallest size they had. His gaze ticked to Lyra and away, then back, because he had never been a coward about the things that could cut him. "Cassian's apartments are two levels up and a hymn east. He isn't wearing the harness. He gave me tea. He asked if the boy from Vireth still had manners."

Lyra said, "And you said." Orin said he had said the boy had worse. He reached inside the coat. Ren's heat-sense flared on instinct and found no blade, only paper and a cold knot of Church iron. "I have two things. You can hit me after the second one."

The first thing was a ring of hymn-keys, black iron, each tooth cut in the shape of a note from the dusk-liturgy. Ren had seen keys like that on inquisitors' belts in Vireth, when the wellgate still thought it owned him. These were heavier. Spire keys. Vault keys. The kind of keys a Solar Saint was not supposed to hand to a fallen Hale who had come back with a story. Orin held them out on his palm as if they might bite him, and Lyra took them without touching his skin.

The second thing was a letter. The paper was academy-cheap, folded small, and it had been opened and closed enough times that the crease was dirty. Ren knew that crease. He had watched Orin keep it when he burned Damar Hale's pardon in front of them at Twin Hearth. Private paper. Father-paper, or the nearest wound. Orin did not offer it to Ren. He offered it to the space between them, which was worse. "I copied the vault seals onto the back," Orin said. "Solenne's been inlaying a dish under the six. Reverse pull. If she chains them, it won't shine. It will drink. She built a mouth for you, Ren. I was going to bring this and the keys and go back up before you arrived, which would have been cleaner." He looked at the water around his boots. "I didn't go back up."

That was the stay. It sat in the cistern with them, plain as a third person. Orin could come down into the dark and be their genius again, Glassheart and guilt and the sister-shaped hole, and stop wearing a Church coat that did not fit. Ren felt the old, stupid wanting rise in him, the slum wanting, the wanting that had shared a shard-crumb with a three-legged dog because keeping it would have made him a noble in miniature. He could say *stay* and mean it. He had a history of meaning it at the wrong hours.

Lyra punched Orin once. She did not telegraph it. She had been an inquisitor; telegraphing was for people who wanted applause. The blow caught him on the same jaw as the bruise and turned his face, and Cinder barked, offended on every possible side. Orin did not lift a hand. He spat a bead of blood into the water and waited, which was, Ren thought, the first correct thing he had done with his body all month. "That was for the court," Lyra said. Her voice was dry enough to sand a plank. "The stay is not yours to offer. You are still in their house. If you vanish down here, Solenne will snuff the cisterns and call it a hymn. You will take your pretty keys' absence back to Cassian with a story, and you will keep his door from locking on us while we go down." She flexed her fingers once, as if checking they still belonged to her. "I did not walk a child into a coffin so you could get sentimental in a sewer."

Ren wanted to tell her that the punch had been a little hard. He also wanted to tell Orin that the punch had been a little soft. He did neither. He took the letter because someone had to, and because Orin's hand had started to shake in the small way that meant Glassheart was leaking. The back of the page was a map in a handwriting too neat for the situation: six coffins, a dish, a spiral, a cell at the top marked warm. On the front, Damar Hale's private script offered a son's pardon if the son brought home a boy in a cage. Ren folded it so he would not have to keep seeing the word *cage*. "I'll keep this until you're allowed to burn it," he said. "Don't die in a coat that ugly. Cinder would be embarrassed."

Orin nodded, once, like a man accepting a sentence he had written himself. He touched Cinder's ear, then Ren's shoulder, then nothing, and he went back toward the ladder that led to Cassian's hymn-east apartments. He did not say goodbye. Ren had noticed that the people who meant to come back often forgot the word, and he decided, against evidence, that this was one of those times. He looked at Lyra. She was already turning toward the deeper dark, keys in her fist, crown whispering in the coat, and he followed with Cinder at his heel because the vault was down, and down was the only direction left that was not a cage.

*

Orin climbed with the taste of blood in his mouth and the Church coat hanging wrong where Lyra's knuckles had landed. The almost-stay went with him like a second bruise. He had stood in the water and felt the old shape of the three of them assemble, Ren's stupid jokes and Lyra's sabers and the dog's ridiculous loyalty, and for a span of heartbeats he had been willing to throw the spy's work down the grate and be Hale-the-traitor in truth. It would have been easier than tea with Cassian. Easier than watching a man with a shard sewn into his chest ask, softly, whether Ren still had manners, as if manners were the wound.

Cassian had given him the hymn-keys without a fight. That was the part Orin would not put in any letter. The Solar Saint had been sitting in a room with the harness on a chair like a shed skin, the well-shard under his sternum ticking at a domestic pace, and he had looked at Orin and said, *If I keep these, she will use me to lock him in. I have been used enough.* Then he had poured tea with a hand that trembled only when the cup was full. Orin had taken the keys and hated how simple it was, hated that mercy from a puppet could feel like a door, hated that he would have to go back now and lie to that same man about a cistern that had been empty.

He paused at the landing and pressed his tongue to the cut inside his cheek. Below, through stone, he felt Ren's heat start to move, Lyra's colder presence beside it, Cinder a small animal spark. They were going down. The letter was in Ren's coat. The dish was waiting. Solenne would feel the seventh the way a locked house felt a broken window. Orin put his court face on, the one that had convinced Damar and Cassian and, once, himself, and he walked toward the apartments with a story about lost keys and frost in the pipes. He did not look back at the grate. Looking back was how you stayed.
