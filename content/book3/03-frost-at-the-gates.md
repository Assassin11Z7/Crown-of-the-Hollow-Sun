# Chapter 3
## Frost at the Gates

Mother Ash came over the outer farms on foot because horses died of her, and because a queen who borrowed a mount looked like a person who needed one. The frost-line ran with her like a hem. Behind her the Nightland host filled the black fields: hollow beasts with lantern ribs, wolves that bowed when she glanced their way, men and women of packed rime who had once been travelers and had asked, in the old manner, to be kept. Cindrel's north gate rose ahead, a mouth of basalt and well-iron, and the Church had already lit the wall-braziers. Stored heat. Tithe heat. Other people's winters stacked into a weapon and pointed at her face.

She did not smile. Smiling was a sun habit. She tasted the city instead, the way she always tasted cities, on the backs of her teeth. Emberwell at the center, fat and mean. Spire above it, singing scarcity. Cisterns under it, wet. And in the cisterns, moving down, a hearth she had already named. Little hearth. Ren Kael, who had sworn to her that he would not restore a sun that belonged to anyone, and who had then gone and become interesting enough that the Church wanted him bottled and the court wanted him crowned and she wanted him seated, simply, in a hall of ice where the cold would finally have a place to put its hands.

The first volley came off the walls as spears of calorancy, gold at the tips, Church-white at the cores. She lifted two fingers. The spears hit a pane of still air and went out with the sound of wet wicks. Her front rank did not break. A hollow stag lowered its antlers, which were not antlers so much as the memory of branching, and scraped a groove in the frozen road as if marking a claim. Mother Ash walked in that groove up to the range where human arrows mattered and stopped, because she had not come to erase a capital. She had come to collect a child before the capital taught him to be a furnace.

Three heats opposed her, and she counted them the way a miser counted coins. The Church on the walls: Solenne's dusk, measured, cruel, competent. The court in its inner rings: a retired saint and a Hale boy telling lies with his shoulders, small warmths trying not to be seen. And the ash-quarters, the uncounted third, where commoners had taken the lesser wellgates and were holding them with pots and bodies and a singing that carried even to her, thin as wire. A three-way siege, then, though only one of the three understood it was a siege. The Church thought it was a defense. The poor thought it was a riot. Mother Ash knew a triangulation when she stood in one. She could have cracked the gate in a dozen breaths. She did not. Cracking the gate would pour her host into streets full of children, and she had not spent a thousand years learning the names of the frozen just to add a city's worth in an afternoon.

"Hold the farms," she said, and the rime-people held. "Eat the tithe-fires on the wall if they throw them. Do not eat the houses." A wolf looked at her with a question in its empty sockets. "The hearth is already inside. We are the door he can still choose. Doors do not stamp."

She put her palm on the north gate's iron. Frost climbed the rivets in ferns. Inside the metal she felt Church seals waking, Solenne's work, old and new, and under those a deeper machinery, a dish, a drinking. The woman in the Spire was preparing to snuff the boy rather than let him be claimed. Mother Ash's mouth went thin. She had offered Ren sanctuary. He had refused a crown of ice with the same stubbornness he refused a crown of gold. That was why she liked him. It was also why she was here, inconveniently, as an army. If he would not sit in her hall she would at least keep the Church from turning him into a cautionary wick.

On the wall a novice shouted a hymn that was supposed to drive frost-spirits back a hundred years. Mother Ash listened to the end out of courtesy. Then she breathed out, and the hymn's heat became a white dust and fell on the archers' sleeves, and the archers learned, in a practical way, that liturgy was not weather.

Nima had lost her left glove in the crowd two streets back and had not gone after it because going after things in a Cindrel riot was how you lost the other glove, and Toma, and the crate they were standing on. The crate belonged to a tallow-seller who had fled when the north gate bells started. From the top of it Nima could see the lesser wellgate of the ash-quarters, the one the Church had shut at dawn, and the people pressed against it with pans and shoulders and a length of chain that had once been a well-guard's. Toma stood at her hip with his chin up, which was how he stood when he was frightened and refusing to give the fright a chair.

"Again," he said. They sang the Vireth well-song because it was the only song they had that was longer than a street and older than a panic. It was not holy. It was what you sang when the shard-crumbs ran out and you needed the little ones to keep their hands moving. Nima's voice was thin. Toma's was all scrape. Together they made a rope. Around the crate, people began to take it up, first the women who had come from Vireth in the same slow leak of refugees that had carried Nima and Toma after Ren's noon, then strangers with Cindrel accents, then a boy with a burned mouth who did not know the words and hummed the shape. Heat did not come from the singing. Nima was not a fool. Heat came from bodies packed together and from the last honest braziers in the quarter and from the fact that if you gave a crowd a meter it stopped crushing itself long enough to be a crowd. Still. The air immediately around the crate stopped biting quite so hard, and Cinder would have liked that, and thinking about Cinder made her throat close, so she sang through it.

A woman in a Church-gray cloak tried to push through to the wellgate with a tithe-seal on a pole. The crowd did not part. Toma, who had once stolen a loaf by looking like a person who had paid, looked like a person who had not paid, and the woman saw the crate and the song and the chain and chose a different street. Nima watched her go and did not feel brave. She felt seventeen streets from home and two friends short. Ren had gone into the capital the way he had gone into every stupid bright thing, and Lyra with him, and the dog, and Nima had followed as far as a girl without Primordial Calor could follow, which turned out to be a crate.

"North gate," Toma said, between verses. He pointed with his chin. Over the roofs the sky had gone the color of a licked tin cup. Frost was climbing the outer walls in sheets. Something with too many joints moved along the battlements and did not fall when the Church threw gold at it. "That's her. The queen. The one from the stories with the wolves."

"She's here for him," Nima said. She did not say Ren's name in a crowd that might sell a name for a coal. "Everybody's here for him. That's the problem with being a boy who lights the sky. You collect armies like fleas."

Toma snorted, which was his laugh when he could not afford a full one, and they picked the song up at the chorus because the little ones at the edge of the crush had started to cry and crying wasted heat. Nima kept her ungloved hand in her armpit and thought, very clearly, that if Ren were here he would have given her his gloves and made a joke about it and then done something ruinous and kind. He was not here. He was under the city, if the rumors about cisterns were worth anything. She sang anyway, because singing was what they had, and because a three-legged dog she loved was down there too, and because the alternative was listening to the wall-bells until her bones learned the tune of being hunted.

The singing was on the fourth repeat when the ground changed its mind. Nima felt it first in the crate, a shiver that was not frost and not crowd. The wellgate's shutters sucked inward, just a finger's width, as if a huge throat under Cindrel had inhaled. All over the ash-quarters the last braziers leaned their flames toward the Spire and then down, down, as if noon had been told to kneel. People screamed in a practical way and grabbed their children. Toma grabbed Nima's sleeve. Over the roofs the Hollow Spire showed a seam of gold that did not rise. It drained. It went into the stone like water into a crack.

Nima did not know the word for what Solenne had just done. She knew the feeling from Vireth, from the night the wellgate ruptured and Ren came up gold and terrified. This was the opposite of that night. This was a mouth opening under the city to drink a boy. She hauled Toma closer on the crate, and she put the song back in her mouth because the little ones were still listening, and because if the world was going to be snuffed she would not let it happen in silence. The frost at the gates kept climbing. The Church kept its bells. Under their feet the Vault of Cinders, which Nima had never seen and would not have named, stood open like a held breath that had finally been allowed to speak.
