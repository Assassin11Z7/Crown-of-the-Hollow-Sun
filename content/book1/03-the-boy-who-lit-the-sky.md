# Chapter 3
## The Boy Who Lit the Sky

The cistern water came to Ren's knees and had a skin of ice that broke in plates and cut the cloth. He waded with Nima on his back and Toma's fist in his collar and Cinder swimming in ugly, determined circles, her stump of a leg paddling as if it had not received the news. The brick throat went on longer than any alley in Cinderrow. Breath steamed and then did not, which meant the cold down here was the old kind, well-adjacent, the kind that lived in stone and waited. Somewhere above them the gold would be dying out of the sky. He could feel it going the way you feel a fever break, and he was not grateful. The fever had at least been a direction.

They crawled up onto a ledge where the city had stacked dry barrels and a rusted grate that still smelled faintly of well-steam. Toma rolled onto his back and laughed once, no humor in it, a sound like a boot coming off. Nima slid from Ren's shoulders and sat with her yellow fingertips in her armpits. Cinder shook the cistern out of her coat and then stood dripping, watching Ren's ribs as if the broken brand might try something else. Ren pulled his shirt up. The pale seam had gone from a scar to a split, edges dark, no blood, a heat-shadow under the skin that ticked when he breathed. "Don't," Toma said. "I wasn't going to poke it," Ren said. "I was going to swear at it." He swore at it. The heat-shadow did not care.

Nima unwrapped a heel of bread from inside her coat. It was flat, gray, the public-trough loaf from before the eleven-day cut, hard enough to hammer a nail. She broke it in four by putting it against the brick and hitting it with the heel of her boot, which was the correct tool. One piece for Toma, one for Ren, one for her, and the last she held out to Cinder until Cinder took it as politely as a three-legged dog in a cistern could manage. "You had bread," Ren said. "You had a crumb," Nima said. "I was waiting." She chewed with her eyes on the split in his ribs and not on his face, which was a kindness. The bread tasted of lime and old yeast and the particular sadness of Church charity. He ate all of it because she had waited through a riot to give it to him, and because the thing in his chest was still hunting for a way out, and food was a way of telling his body it still belonged to him.

Boots, above. Not the crowd. The crowd had been a river. This was a count, two-three, two-three, the purge-squad pace even after the squad on the wall had become a smell. Light came down the next throat, white and dome-shaped, a heat-shield walked along the water like a moving room. Church primer-form. Ren had seen it an hour ago on a man who was now not a man. He saw it better now because he had nothing else to look at and because the thing in his ribs leaned toward it the way Cinder leaned toward meat. The dome was a spent well-thread woven in a pattern of six, overlapping, a clerk's knot. He could see the knot. He could see where the man inside it was wasting heat at the join. He could see, with a clarity that made him sick, how you would do it if you did not want to waste anything.

"They're sweeping the mouths," Toma whispered. "If we go back we're in the street. If we go forward we're in them." Nima's bread-hand had gone still. Cinder's hackles were a ridged line. Ren put his palm on the split and felt the heat-shadow turn under the skin, interested, rude as a guest who opens cupboards. He did not want a fight. He wanted a wall. He wanted the primer-form the way he had wanted the crumb, a small stolen thing to keep the people next to him from dying in the next ten breaths. He held the pattern in his head, six overlapping threads, the clerk's knot, and he asked the broken brand to copy it. He did not know he was asking. He found out when the air in front of the ledge went dense and gold-white and became a dome that did not waste anything at the join.

It was the same shield. It was not the same shield. The Churchman's dome had been a room you rented. This one sat on the ledge as if it had been poured there and left to set, a heat so even the cistern ice along its edge receded in a clean circle and did not steam itself to nothing. Toma made a sound. Nima put her bread down. Inside the dome the air was the temperature of a held hand. Ren stared at what he had done and felt the copy lock in, a click behind the eyes, the way a stolen route locked in once you had walked it. He would be able to do this again. He understood that without being told. He also understood, in the same unwelcome click, that the leftover heat had not liked being asked for only a wall.

The overflow went up. Not a sheet this time, not the gold sky. A column, tight, through the rusted grate, through the brick, into the streets above as if the streets had been chimneys waiting. Ren tried to pinch it off and got a fraction, which was better than the Wellgate and still not control. The column hit the frost that lived in Cinderrow, in the tallow-yards, in the Stacks, in the eleven marked days of shut troughs, and the frost gave up. He heard it as a long crack, then as dripping, then as voices starting in a dozen alleys at once, the particular shouting of people who have been cold so long that warmth feels like an attack. Toma was on his feet. "That's us," he said. "That's you. That's the whole slum hearing you." "I noticed," Ren said. The dome held. The column went on, thinner, a leak he could not find the valve for.

They did not stay to be swept. He dropped the shield because keeping it was a lantern in a dark throat, and they waded west, away from the counted boots, Cinder swimming, Nima on his back again, Toma cursing the ice plates. The cisterns opened into a junction where other runaways had already gathered, a dozen, more, faces from the Wellgate crowd and faces that had only followed the gold. A woman with a tithe-clerk's ink still on her fingers looked at the split light coming down from the grates, at the meltwater threading the brick, and said the name as if she were putting a stone on a grave. "Solenne will come," she said. "Cardinal Solenne. She came for the last one. She'll come for this." No one asked what the last one had been. In Vireth you learned the stories of false suns the way you learned which alleys froze first: late, and from someone else's mouth, and too useful to ignore.

Ren knew the outline because everyone did. The Church of the Last Ember taught that the god was dead and his heat was a finite mercy, and that anyone who burned too bright was a thief stealing the years off the rest. False suns got named, hunted, extinguished. The last one people would say out loud had been a girl. Twelve. A river city south of Vireth. The Cardinal had gone herself. The clerk-woman had more, and she gave it because fear likes to be specific. "She loved the one before the girl," she said. "Halvern. Vessel four. She put him out with her own hands and then she put the girl out and she did not stop being Cardinal. That is the kind of salvation she sells. Managed dusk. No one too warm. No one too bright. You—" she looked at Ren's steam, at the meltwater, at the gold still ghosting the edges of him, "—you lit the sky. She will not manage you. She will end you."

Ten streets. He did not have the number until they came up through a cistern mouth in the tallow-yard and saw it. Frost that had been black in the mortar was running. Eaves were dripping. People stood in doorways with their hands out, not quite daring to believe, and the Hollow Sun hung over it all unchanged, black disk, cold light, a lid that had not agreed to any of this. The thaw ran from the Wellgate down through Cinderrow and across the Stacks in a band you could have walked in an hour, ten streets of slum that steamed like a noble garden and would freeze again the moment he left, and he knew that too, the way he knew the shield now, a click, a cruelty. Nima got down from his back. Her yellow fingertips were less yellow. She looked at the dripping eaves and then at him, and the look was not worship. It was the look she had given the crumb. Inventory. Cost.

"You can't stay in the Kiln," Toma said. He had understood it faster than Ren wanted him to. "They'll come to the Kiln. They'll come to Mrs. Pell. They'll come to anyone who stood in that circle." Ren wiped melt off his mouth with the back of his wrist. The split on his ribs ticked. Cinder sat on his foot, which was not going to be allowed to remain a strategy. "So I don't go back," he said. "You two do. You take her." He meant Cinder and Nima both and Toma knew it. "If I was a hero I'd make a speech. I'm not. I'm a problem with a dog. You keep the dog. You keep the door-cloth nailed. If the troughs run because of this, you use them. If they don't, you steal. Same as before." Nima said, "Cinder won't stay." Cinder, traitor, leaned her whole weight into Ren's shin and confirmed it. "Fine," Ren said. "The dog's a problem too. You still go back."

Toma's face did the thin thing. He was fourteen and he had been afraid in the Kiln and he had been afraid in the road and he was going to do the right stupid thing if Ren let him. Ren did not let him. He put the last of the copied shield around Toma and Nima, a small even room, and he pointed them at the alley that ran to the Kiln, and he held the shield long enough that walking away from him was easier than walking toward him. "Go," he said. "If I come back and you're frozen I will be so angry." Nima held out the last dust of the bread in her palm, nothing, a gesture. He took the nothing. Then he dropped the shield before the overflow could find them, and he turned into the thawed streets with Cinder at his ruined gait, into a city that had ten warm blocks with his name already moving through them, and above the wet eaves the black disk watched like an eye that had started, just started, to dream it was open.
