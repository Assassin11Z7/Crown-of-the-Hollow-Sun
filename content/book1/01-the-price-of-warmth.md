# Chapter 1
## The Price of Warmth

The crumb came off the wagon-slat with a sound like a snapped fingernail. Ren had his cheek in the slush and his arm in the dark, fingers working the iron grate that kept the Church's leavings from the street. Heat leaked through the metal in a thin, mean thread. He hooked the shard-crumb with a nail and pulled. It was no bigger than a tooth, cloudy white, still holding a little of the well. The wagon lurched. A hoof came down a handspan from his ear and threw dirty ice into his mouth. He rolled, stuffed the crumb inside his shirt against the brand on his ribs, and ran before the driver thought to look under the wheels.

Cinderrow took him the way it always did, by the elbows. The alley was a gutter between kiln-houses, frost on the brick and smoke that never quite became fire. Overhead the Hollow Sun hung like a burned coin, black disk, cold light, the same dead noon it had been since before anyone in the slums had a grandmother. Ren did not look at it. Looking did not make it warmer. He cut left at the tallow-yard, right at the shrine of the Last Ember where the offering bowl held three frozen coppers and a prayer-strip the wind had glued to the ice, and did not stop until the wagon's bells had gone thin behind him. His breath tore. The crumb against his ribs was a small, stubborn heat, and the null-brand under it ate at that heat the way a leak eats a room.

Cinder found him at the turn for the Kiln. The dog came on three legs and a ruined fourth, stump wrapped in a rag Ren had boiled once and never quite gotten clean. She shoved her skull into his knee hard enough to stagger him, then put her nose to his shirt and made a sound that was not a bark and not a whine. "Yeah," Ren said. "I got it. Don't tell the taxmen." He scratched the base of her ear with two fingers, quick, because standing still in Cinderrow was how you learned which of your bones the frost liked. They went together, his boots and her three-beat gait, down the steps into the old firing chamber that had not fired anything in living memory.

Toma had the door-cloth pinned with a nail. He lifted it with his shoulder and did not ask where Ren had been, which was how Toma said he had been afraid. He was fourteen and already going thin in the face the way slum boys did when the public troughs ran short. Nima sat on the brick lip of the dead kiln with her feet off the floor, wrapping and unwrapping a scrap of wool around her left hand. She was eleven. She looked at Ren's shirt, then at Cinder, then at Ren again, and the looking was a kind of inventory she had learned too early. "You're late," she said. "Toma said you froze in a ditch." "Toma's an optimist," Ren said. He sat. The Kiln held a sour, used-up warmth, the ghost of a thousand firings, not enough to keep the water in the pail unskinned. He took the crumb out.

They did it the way they always did. Ren cupped the shard-crumb in both palms and breathed on it, not because breath helped, but because it made the moment last. Calorancy was a noble word for spending heat, and he had no technique and no training and no right, only a crumb and three other bodies that needed it. The white tooth glowed the color of old coals. He held it out. Toma put his hands over Ren's. Nima put her wrapped hand in last, and Cinder shoved her muzzle into the gap and would not be moved. The heat went through them in a slow, ugly pulse, more ache than comfort, the way blood comes back into a dead foot. Nima made a small noise and then shut her mouth on it. Toma stared at the crumb as if staring could keep it from dying. Ren watched their faces and not the light, because the light was going to go out and the faces were the point.

When the crumb was only a cloudy pip, he cracked it between his molars and swallowed the last of it, which was a waste and also the rule. You did not leave heat on the floor for the frost to take. The pip sat in his gut like a coal the size of a lie. He took Nima's hand without asking and unwrapped the wool. Two fingertips had gone the dull yellow that came before black. He put them in his mouth and held them there, which was disgusting and worked. Nima looked at the kiln wall and did not pull away. "Mrs. Pell's boy didn't come back last night," Toma said, too casual. "The trough on Pell Street froze to the valves. They say the Church took the night allotment for the Saint's procession." Ren spat Nima's fingers out and wrapped them again, tighter. "The Saint can process in the dark," he said. "He's got a shard in his chest. He'll live."

Cassian Vale, Solar Saint, puppet of the Last Ember, walked the upper city on feast days with a well-shard sewn under the skin above his heart so the faithful could watch him steam. Ren had seen it once from a drain grate, the procession, the white heat coming off the man's breastbone like a second, smaller, nastier sun. Down here the troughs froze and Mrs. Pell's boy went out for scrap and did not come back. Toma picked at a scab on his wrist. "There's talk," he said. "Wellgate. People from the Stacks and the tallow-yards and even some from the Bone Market. They're going to stand in the road and not move until the night allotment comes back." Nima's yellow fingertips flexed inside the wool. "Stand in the road and get counted for the next procession," Ren said. "As fuel." He meant it as a joke. It came out the other way.

Cinder turned a circle on the brick and lay down with her stump tucked in, which was her way of saying the argument could go on without her. Ren put his palm on her ribs and felt the three-legged engine of her going. The Kiln ticked as the frost worked the old mortar. Somewhere above them a bell marked the cold hour, the one the Church called Vespers of Ash, when the public troughs were supposed to run a last thin thread of well-water and had not, now, for eleven days. He knew the count because Nima kept it on the wall in charcoal, eleven marks, a child's ledger of being failed. "We should go," Toma said. He did not look at Ren when he said it. "If there's enough of us they can't take everyone." "They don't need everyone," Ren said. "They need a lesson. Lessons are cheap. People are cheaper." Nima slid off the kiln lip. Her boots were two sizes past useful, stuffed with rag. "If we don't go," she said, "and they get the allotment back, we weren't there. If we don't go and they don't, we sat here. I'm tired of sitting here."

Ren looked at her yellow fingertips and at Toma's thin face and at the eleven marks on the wall. The pip in his gut had already gone out. The null-brand on his ribs, pale as a scar and colder than the rest of him, gave its usual dull complaint, the old church charity that was supposed to ward frost-rot and had never done anything but ache. He had been told, by a woman who might have been a nurse and might have been a thief, that the brand was a kindness put on a foundling so the Nightlands could not smell him. He had been told a lot of things. Kindness, in Vireth, usually had a receipt. "You're both idiots," he said, and stood, and Cinder stood with him, which settled it. He pulled the door-cloth aside. The alley air came in like a blade. "If we die in the road I want it on record that I voted no." "No you didn't," Nima said. She was already wrapping a second scrap around her hand, efficient, eleven years old and done with his mouth.

They went up into Cinderrow as the cold hour thickened. The Hollow Sun had not moved. It never moved. It hung over Vireth the way a lid hangs over a pot, black, light without heat, and the city lived in that lid-light the way mushrooms live in a cellar. Smoke stood in columns from the noble stacks on the rim, where the well-shards were hoarded in iron caskets and calorancers spent heat like coin, making rooms that steamed and gardens that should not have existed and little indoor noons for people who had never chewed a crumb. Down here the smoke was only the idea of smoke. Ren walked with Nima's good hand hooked in the back of his belt and Toma on his other side and Cinder ranging ahead on her three legs, and the rumor came to meet them before they had cleared the tallow-yard. It came as voices, then as bodies, then as a current in the street that took the ankles and did not ask.

A woman from the Stacks had a child on her hip and a broken trough-ladle in her fist, the iron head still filmed with the last well-ice. A Bone Market cutter walked with his boning knives peace-tied, which meant nothing except that he wanted to be seen following a rule while he broke a larger one. Tithe-clerks in ash-gray had come down from the middle terraces, which surprised Ren more than the knives. When the clerks starved, the math had gone wrong in public. Toma said, under his breath, "That's more than the Stacks." Ren said, "That's enough to make someone nervous," and did not add that nervous Churchmen lit people like kindling and called it order. He kept Nima close and Cinder closer and let the current take them toward the Wellgate because getting out of it now would have meant going backward through a thousand shoulders, and Nima's fingers were still yellow, and Mrs. Pell's boy was still not back.

The Wellgate showed itself as a wall before it showed itself as a gate. The emberwell of Vireth sat in a bowl of black stone older than the city, older than the Church, a wound in the ground where the dead god's leftover heat still welled up to be bottled and sold and prayed over. The gate was bronze and frost-welded iron, twice as high as a kiln-house, stamped with the Last Ember's disk. On feast days it opened to let the Saint through. On ordinary days it opened to let the tithe-wagons out, crumbs falling through the slats for boys with their cheeks in the slush. Today it was shut, and the shutness had a crowd in front of it, and the crowd had a sound that was not a prayer and not a song. Ren felt Nima's hook in his belt tighten. He felt the pip's ghost in his gut. He felt the brand on his ribs tick once, sharp, as if it had smelled the well.

Guards on the wall. Not city watch. Purge-squad white, the Church's own, calorancy already steaming off their gauntlets in little wasteful curls. That was money burning in the open air, a warning written in heat. Toma swore, a small filthy word he saved for real trouble. Ren should have turned them around then. He knew it the way he knew which alleys froze first. He looked at the gate and at the white gauntlets and at Nima's wool-wrapped hand, and he did not turn. "Stay on my left," he said. "If it breaks, we go for the cistern mouths, not the terraces. Cinder, heel." The dog came back and sat on his boot, which was as close to heel as she had ever come. The crowd packed forward another step, and the bronze disk on the gate threw back the Hollow Sun's cold light, and Ren went with them because the people he would not leave were going, and because the well was right there, a whole city's worth of heat behind a door, and he was seventeen and tired of chewing crumbs.
