# Chapter 2
## Riot at the Wellgate

The first thing thrown was the ladle. It went up from the Stacks woman with the child on her hip, iron head turning once in the lid-light, and hit the bronze disk on the Wellgate with a sound that ran the wall. The child started crying. The crowd took the sound and made it larger. Ren had Nima's hook in his belt and Toma's shoulder under his hand and Cinder's ruff in his other fist, and none of that was enough when the street packed forward and the people behind them decided the people in front were a door. He got an elbow in the mouth. He tasted old iron. "Left," he said, and no one heard him, and he said it again into Nima's hair, "cistern mouths, left," as if repeating it could cut a path through a thousand bodies that had waited eleven days for a trough.

The purge squad did not wait for a second throw. Heat came off the wall in a sheet, calorancy spent the expensive way, a warning written on skin. The front rank of the crowd went down as if the street had been pulled from under them. Steam burst from coats. Someone's prayer-strip caught and became a little running fire and then nothing. Ren shoved Nima behind his hip and took the edge of the sheet on his shoulder. It should have cooked him. It cooked the cloth and left the brand on his ribs screaming, a cold so sharp it was a sound. Toma was shouting. Cinder had her teeth in a stranger's coat because the stranger was treading on her stump. The Wellgate did not open. The bronze disk threw the Hollow Sun back at them, black light on burning wool, and the squad on the wall spent another sheet, narrower, aimed, the kind of shot you used on a man and not a mob.

Ren saw the aimed shot coming the way you see a cart when you have already stepped into the road. It was a white bar, pretty as a saint's bone, and it was going through the Stacks woman and the child and then through whatever stood behind them, which was Toma. Ren did the stupid thing because the stupid thing was the only size that fit. He let go of Cinder and shoved Toma and put his own chest in the bar. The heat hit the null-brand like a hammer hitting a locked box. For a blink there was nothing, a hole in the world the shape of his ribs. Then the box broke.

It did not feel like power. It felt like a stove door coming open in a room that had been freezing for a thousand years. The brand on his ribs split down the pale seam, not blood, not light, a heat with no color he had a name for. It went out through his bones and into the street and did not ask him. The white bar from the wall met it and vanished, not blocked, eaten. Ren's mouth was open. He was not shouting. He could not find the air. The crowd nearest him fell back because falling back was what you did when a boy cooked the shot that should have killed him and kept standing, steam coming off his shirt in a column that did not thin.

The sky went gold. Not sunrise. There had not been a sunrise in Ashveil since the god died and left his heat in the ground for men to bottle. This was the lid coming off. The Hollow Sun's black disk sat in a gold that had no business being there, cold light drowned in a color like the inside of a kiln at full roar, and the Wellgate's bronze threw that gold back in a sheet that made the purge squad throw their hands up as if against a glare they had only read about in the forbidden psalms. Ren heard Nima say his name. He heard Cinder. He heard, very small, the pip in his gut that was not a pip anymore, that was a well, that was a dead god's leftover and also something older, and it was using his hands.

He tried to shut it. He tried the way you try to shut a door in a wind. The heat did not care. It ran up the wall along the white curls of the squad's wasted calorancy and found the men who had spent it. Gauntlets burst. The first guard came off the parapet on fire that did not look like fire, gold at the core, and hit the bronze disk and slid. The second tried a heat-shield, the Church primer-form, a dome of spent well-light, and the overflow went through the dome as if the dome were paper and the man inside it were paper too. Ren shouted *stop* and heard it come out as nothing. He got to his knees. He put both palms on the stones and tried to push the heat back into the brand, into the box, into the foundling kindness that had been a seal. The stones went dark-wet and then dry-white. Frost that had lived in the mortar since the last true winter ran out of the joints as water and then as steam.

The rest of the squad died in a single ugly spill. Eight men. He counted because his mind had grabbed counting the way a hand grabs a rail. Eight men and their heat and their white and the little wasteful curls, gone into gold and then into a smell like a tallow-yard on a bad day. The crowd had a hole in it now, a circle of cleared stone around Ren, and in that circle the air shimmered the way it did over the noble stacks. Toma was on his back ten paces off, alive, mouth open. Nima had both hands over her ears. Cinder stood in front of Ren on three legs and barked at the gold sky as if barking could put the lid back on. The Wellgate's bronze had begun to run. Not melt, not yet, but run, a sag in the disk where a saint's face had been.

Someone in the crowd said *false sun* in the voice people used for plague. Someone else started a prayer and lost it in the middle. Ren looked at his hands and they were only hands, scabbed, a little wet, the knuckles split from the elbow in his mouth. He did not feel like a sun. He felt like a cracked grate with something shoving through. The thing in his ribs had a hunger that was not his, a reaching up toward the black disk as if the disk were a face it knew. He folded over Cinder and put his forehead on her neck and told her, very quietly, to move, and she would not, because she had decided that the gold was a problem you barked at until it went away.

A woman came through the hole in the crowd without seeming to hurry. Two sabers, unshined, the kind of steel you keep for work. The blades had seals burned into them in a script Ren did not know, black marks that ate the gold at their edges and made a thin, ugly smoke. She was older than him by a handful of years, ash-coat open, inquisitor's cut to the cloth and no inquisitor's badge at the throat. She looked at the sagging gate and at the eight cooked men and at him, and her face did the small professional thing of putting horror in a drawer to be felt later. "Down," she said, as if he were a dog. Cinder snarled. Ren tried to say he was not doing it. The heat answered for him, a pulse that hit her sabers and died on the seals with a sound like spit on a stove.

She came on anyway. That was the part he would remember when he could remember anything: she came on into the overflow with her seals eating gold and her mouth a thin line, and when she was close enough to cut she did not cut. She put the flat of a saber against the air in front of his chest and the seal-script flared and the heat in him flinched, a whole well flinching, and for one clean second he could breathe. "Who put that in you," she said. Not a question that wanted an answer. A question that wanted a name she could hunt. Ren laughed, which was a mistake, because laughing let the heat up through his teeth in a gold thread. "Nobody asked me," he said. The saber-flat slipped. The gold came back.

He did the second stupid thing of the hour. He grabbed Cinder's ruff and Nima's wrist and hauled, and the overflow went behind him like a door slamming, a shove of heat that threw the dual-saber woman two steps and bowled the nearest rioters into each other, and he ran for the cistern mouths the way he had said he would before any of this had a name. Toma was up and coming. Good. The street was a wreck of steam and yelling and the Wellgate sagging in its frame like a drunk. Gold stood in the sky over Vireth, a color the city had no right to, and people in the terraces would be at their windows, and the Church would already be naming this a heresy, and none of that was as loud as Nima's pulse in the wrist he was holding.

They hit the cistern stairs in a pack of strangers who had the same idea, down into the brick throat where the city kept its dark water. The gold did not follow all the way. It thinned as the stone closed over, and with the thinning came the cold, ordinary and huge, and Ren felt the thing in his ribs hunt for a way out and not find one, which was a mercy he did not trust. Cinder's claws skated on the wet. Toma was swearing in a loop. Nima had not said a word since his name.

Behind them, up in the lid-light, a woman with two sabers would be getting to her feet, and a Cardinal would be hearing the word *vessel*, and the eight men on the wall would still be dead. Ren kept going down because down was the only direction that did not have a sky in it.
