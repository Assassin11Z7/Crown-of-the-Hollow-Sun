# Chapter 3
## The Solar Saint

Cassian Vale walked toward the dais on feet that were not entirely his. The shard in his chest kept a second stride, a heat-count that did not match his heart, and the court of Cindrel clapped in time with the wrong one. Gold leaf had been glued to the vault ribs for the occasion, a cheap noon for a city that had not seen a true one in a thousand years. Nobles in well-shard furs lined the nave of the Cathedral of the Last Ember and tried to look as if they had always believed in saints that could be manufactured. Cassian had been a choir scholar six weeks ago, a boy with a decent voice and a talent for kneeling. Now he wore white that itched and a sun stitched in black thread over the place where they had opened him.

Cardinal Solenne waited at the top of the steps with her hands folded in the manner of someone who did not need to hold a weapon to have one. She was soft-spoken even when the cathedral was full. That was the trick of her: she made the air lean in, and leaning in felt like consent. "Kneel, Cassian," she said, and he knelt because the shard liked her voice more than it liked his spine. A deacon brought the circlet, iron darkened to look like old night, set with a single emberwell chip that would have bought a Vireth street for a winter. Solenne set it on his hair. It was heavier than theology. "The world has grown a fever," she told the court, and the court made the small sacred noises courts make when they are being given permission to be afraid. "A boy in the outer dark spends heat as if heat were water. We will not answer him with another flood. We will answer him with a river that knows its banks. Behold the Solar Saint. Managed dusk. A sun that belongs to the Church, which is to say, to you."

He felt the stitch-line pull when he breathed. They had sewn the shard behind his sternum in a room that smelled of boiled thread and incense, Solenne watching with the mild interest of a woman who had done this before and had opinions about the ones who had failed. Vessel four had been Halvern. Cassian knew the name the way you knew a ghost that still received mail. Solenne had loved Halvern; the love was a rumor with good shoes. She did not love Cassian. She had selected him. Selection was cleaner. The shard was a well-fragment older than the cathedral, cut from something that had once been a god's leftover heat, and it did not care that he had wanted to finish a commentary on scarcity hymns. It cared that he was empty enough to hold it.

When she bid him stand, he stood, and when she bid him show them, he opened his hands. Heat unrolled from the stitch, gold and obedient, a canopy of tame noon that warmed the first six pews and left the poor in the back as cold as doctrine. The nobles sighed as if they had been fed. Cassian kept his face in the expression he had practiced: serenity, which was panic with its collar buttoned. He could feel, at the far edge of the shard's hearing, another heat moving in the west, messy and alive, a boy who did not ask pews for permission. The shard hated that boy the way a locked door hates a thief. Cassian did not yet know if he hated him too. He suspected the hate would be provided, like the cloak.

After the rite they took him to a vestry and sat him on a bench while a physician checked the stitches and a secretary read a list of settlements that had begun to say a name wrong. Thornveil. The pass. Boy Who Brought Noon. Solenne stood at the window and watched Cindrel's emberwells pulse under their iron hats, the city's slow, rationed heartbeat. "You will not chase him yet," she said, as if Cassian had been about to run. "You will sit in the sun we have given you and let the outer roads come to us frightened. When they are frightened enough, we will send you as a kindness. If you meet him, you will not debate. You will extinguish. A second sun is not a mercy. It is a war the land cannot afford." She turned, and her softness had an edge you could have shaved with. "Halvern thought he could bargain with what lived in him. Do not make me write your name in the same tense."

Cassian touched the cloth over the shard and felt it answer like a coal poked. He said the words she wanted: he was the Church's dusk, he would not flood, he understood banks. Inside the words, a smaller voice, still choir-trained, wondered what it would be like to spend heat on a whole street just to see if the poor in the back pews had faces. He put that voice in a box. Puppets who rummaged in their own boxes did not keep their stitches.

Outside, Cindrel rang the noon-bell for a noon that had not happened, and the court went home to tell itself it had been saved.

Orin Hale read the letter twice on the lee side of a windbreak while Ren slept with his mouth open and Lyra pretended not to watch the road. The courier had been a Hale man in a coat with the crest picked out, which was how fallen houses announced they still remembered needles. He had not given his name. He had given a packet sealed with Damar's ring and a look that said *your father is still your father whether the academy struck you or not*, and then he had gone back into the dark as if the Nightlands were a corridor in the old manse. Orin broke the seal with a thumbnail and hated how steadily his hands chose to work.

Damar's public hand was the one the courts would recognize: House Hale, diminished, offered a full pardon for the prodigy's desertion, restoration of the Glassheart stipend, a return to the inner ring, if Orin Hale delivered the unlicensed sun to Church custody in Cindrel. The language was clean as a blade wiped after use. *Deliver.* Not kill. Not even bind. Deliver, as if Ren were a parcel that had gotten legs. There would be a writ. There would be a saint. There would be, Damar wrote, an end to the embarrassment of a Hale standing in the weather with slum-heat and an excommunicated inquisitor. Orin could smell the drawing-room in the ink, the cold parlor where Lirae's portrait still hung because taking it down would have required someone to admit she had frozen in a house that owned a well and had still not opened it in time.

Under the pardon, folded smaller, a private sheet. Damar's other hand, the one he used when he was a father and not a signature. *Your sister is still in the ground we paid for. Come home before I have to buy a second plot. The boy is not your brother. Do not make me explain this to a cardinal with a saint in her pocket.* No endearment. Damar did not spend those. Orin held both pages and watched Ren's sleeping face, which looked, in unguarded hours, like any seventeen-year-old who had not eaten enough onions. The Primordial Calor made a faint gold thread under the skin of Ren's throat, a tell Lyra hated. Orin had improved Glassheart in three years. Ren had improved it in ten seconds and then used it to warm a cellar of strangers. That was not a theorem. That was a personality, and personalities were how houses died.

Lyra's eyes were on him now, not the road. She had a talent for smelling paper that wanted to become a knife. "Hale," she said, not a question. Orin did not insult her by lying. He said his father had found a courier, that the house still knew where its failed son put his feet. He did not mention the pardon. He did not mention *deliver*. He folded both letters into the lining of his too-fine coat, the public one and the private one sharing a pocket like a married couple who should have divorced. "If he asks me to choose a crest over a person," Orin said, which was a sentence with a hole in it, "I will tell you." Lyra's mouth did the unkind almost-approval she saved for people she had not yet decided to bury. "If you choose the crest," she said, "I will tell you first, with a saber. Sleep. The Glassbridge is two days if the ice is honest."

Ren stirred and asked, without opening his eyes, whether they were talking about him, because people usually were. Orin said they were talking about roads. Ren accepted this the way he accepted stew, with suspicion and hunger in equal parts, and put his hand on Cinder's ribs and went back under. Orin sat with the letters against his heart, which was the wrong place for them, and thought of Lirae in the Hale solarium with frost on her lashes, of Damar's voice in a doorway saying *close the vents, we cannot afford the degrees*, of a boy from Vireth who would have opened every vent in Ashveil and called it manners. The pardon was a door back into a name. The name had not kept Lirae warm. He did not burn the letters. He did not send the courier a reply. He watched the Hollow Sun's disk sit on the dark like a coin no one would pick up, and he kept the offer the way you kept a blade you swore you would not use, which was how blades got used.
