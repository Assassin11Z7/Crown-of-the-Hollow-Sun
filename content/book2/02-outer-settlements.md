# Chapter 2
## Outer Settlements

They came into Thornveil on a road that had given up being a road and become a suggestion of cart-ruts filled with old snow. Ren walked in front because Lyra would not let him walk behind, where she could not watch his hands, and Orin would not let him walk anywhere without reciting the boiling point of ice as if ice had not already made its opinion clear. Cinder hopped between them, ears flat, smelling a village the way a village smelled when it had started spending its own people for degrees. The houses were low and hunched, timber black with tar, roofs wearing frost like a tax. No dogs barked. Dogs had been eaten or had left, which in the outer settlements was the same sentence with different manners.

The tax-well stood in the square under a Church cage of iron and prayer-script, a brick throat with a warden beside it who had the face of a man paid to watch thirst. A null-brand hung on the cage like a dark coin. Ren felt it from twenty paces, a cold that was not weather, the same family of hush that had ruptured at Vireth's Wellgate and poured a sky into him. Lyra's fingers found his sleeve without looking. "Cellars," she said. "Hands. Not that. You look at that brand too long and I will sew your eyelids to your thoughts." The warden looked at them the way ledgers looked at shortages. He had a pike and a brass whistle and the slack posture of someone who believed the Church would always arrive before the dying finished.

A woman came out of the nearest house with a shawl that had been a tapestry in a kinder century. She was the letter, Ren knew, because she held herself like a person who had already spent her last stamp. "Illa said you would come if the flower did not break," she said, and then saw that the flower had broken, and did not let her face do anything expensive about it. Her name was Mera, she told them, and the well had been taking more since the noon at the pass, a new tithe for a new miracle, scarcity dressed as prudence. Children first, then the old, then the ones who still had work in their hands. She led them off the square because the warden's whistle was a theology she did not want to test.

The cellar under Mera's house had been a root-store and was now a chapel of breathing. Nine people lay in a dark that had weight. Ren went down the ladder with Cinder slung under one arm and the Calor already pressing at his mouth like a confession. He did not light the well. He did not light the sky. He sat on the packed earth and took a child's stiff hands and let heat move the way it had moved into Nima's bread in Vireth: privately, without a sermon. The Glassheart helped. Orin's theorem kept the gift from becoming a fire. The child's fingers pinked. Someone started crying in the practical way of people who had been waiting for permission. Lyra stood at the ladder with one saber drawn not for the cold but for the warden, and Orin measured the air with a strip of treated paper that darkened as the degrees returned.

He went from body to body until his own teeth ached with the effort of not doing more. Constraint was a smaller word when it had faces. A boy with a cough took heat and tried to give Ren a button, which Ren refused, which made the boy look betrayed, so Ren took the button and put it in the pocket that was already a museum of other people's mercy. Cinder licked a woman's wrist and was permitted, which was the highest civic honor Thornveil still offered. When the cellar had color in it again, Ren climbed into the lane and the Hollow Sun's disk sat in the dark sky like a closed eye that had nearly opened. He did not look at it long.

Illa found him by the woodpile. She was perhaps twelve, perhaps a hard ten, with a braid pinned up in the old outer fashion and fingers that had learned to make beauty out of weather. She did not curtsy. The outer settlements had spent curtsying on the last generation that could still feel their toes. "You melted the letter-flower," she said, not quite accusing. "I made another. You have to wear it or it doesn't count." She held a circlet of frost-flowers, pale and barbed, each bloom a geometry of ice that would cut a careless thumb. In Vireth a crown was a joke you told about nobles. Here it was a girl spending an hour she did not have on a boy she had decided was real.

Ren bent because she would not reach, and because he had never learned to refuse a child who had already done the work. The frost-flowers sat on his hair and immediately began to consider melting, which would have been a story, so he pulled the heat down out of his scalp until his thoughts felt packed in snow. Illa nodded once, priest of a rite she had invented that afternoon. "Boy Who Brought Noon," she said, trying the name the way you tried a shoe. "That is what they are calling you on the tax-road. The pass was gold. My cousin saw the color on the underside of clouds. You should have a better coat."

Lyra came around the woodpile as if she had been born from it. She looked at the crown, at Illa, at Ren's attempt to be a statue that did not thaw jewelry. "He does not need a better coat," Lyra said. "He needs a muzzle." Then, because Illa's face had done a small wounded thing, Lyra crouched to the girl's height with a creak of old inquisitor leather and spoke more quietly. "It is well made. It will not last on him. He is a stove with opinions." She lifted the circlet off Ren's head before the first petal could become a tear, and the cold of it went into her sealed wrists without complaint. She did not give it back. She slid it inside her coat against the lining, where evidence went when she had not yet decided whether it was evidence.

Illa watched the theft with the gravity of someone who understood keeping. "Don't lose it," she said. "If the Church comes, tell them the flowers were mine. They tax wells. They do not know how to tax a crown that isn't gold." She went back toward the cellar with the walk of a child who had been put in charge of adults and was trying not to enjoy it. Ren stared at the place in Lyra's coat where the frost-flowers had vanished. "You're keeping it," he said. She told him it would melt on his skull and become a rumor with petals, and that she preferred her rumors dry. He did not believe her, not entirely. He had seen her fail to throw away a cracked seal-stone that had belonged to a dead partner. Lyra kept broken things that had been given on purpose.

They worked the other cellars until the village had a pulse that could be mistaken, from a distance, for ordinary winter. Ren copied a warding-heat from a Thornveil grandmother, a trick of holding warmth in bone-marrow the way picklers held brine, and once he had it he could pass it without steam. Orin was delighted in the pinched way of a scholar watching a theft he had to admire. The warden blew his whistle twice when a shutter leaked gold light, and Lyra went into the square and spoke to him in the voice she had used on Bone Market informers, all courtesy and implied graves. The whistle did not blow a third time. The null-brand hung on its cage and dreamed of ruptures it was not offered.

By the unclocked hour that served Thornveil for midnight, the name had settled on him like a second, worse coat. Boy Who Brought Noon. Men said it into their cups of melted snow. Women said it into the hair of children who had stopped shaking. It traveled faster than they would, out along the tax-road toward settlements that still had dogs, toward waystations, toward whatever ear in Cindrel collected miracles for Cardinal Solenne to file under *extinguish*. Ren sat on Mera's stoop with Cinder's head in his lap and felt the Calor turn, satisfied and hungry both, and hated that those could be the same sensation. He had not lit the well. He had not lit the sky. He had still written his name in a dozen warm rooms.

Orin joined him with a map sketched on the back of Mera's letter, roads like cracks, a river marked *Glassbridge* in a hand that had learned calligraphy in a house with a name. "If we stay two days the tithe-wardens will come from the inner ring," Orin said. "If we leave at once, the heat we left will last until the well drinks it back. There is no third arithmetic in which you have saved them. You have postponed a ledger." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. Then he added, because Lirae's absence had taught him to leave a door unlatched, "It was still right. I am only telling you the bill."

Lyra stood in the lane and watched the square, where the warden had gone to sleep against the cage like a man hugging his own unemployment. The frost-flower crown made no shape in her coat, but Ren knew it was there the way he knew Cinder's missing leg was there, an absence that had become an object. "We leave before the disk climbs," she said. "You did not make noon. You made a rumor that thinks it is noon. That will have to be enough for your conscience and too much for the Church." She glanced at him, dry as old bread. "If you want to be a saint, steal a better title. That one will get you caged."

Mera came to the stoop with a packet of hard bread and a warning she had been saving. Riders had been seen on the high road at dusk, not tax-men, not wardens: white cloaks with a sun stitched in black thread, the new colors. A Solar Saint, the rumor said, crowned in Cindrel while Thornveil was busy not dying. A boy or a man, no one agreed, with a shard in him that made the air taste like a struck match. Mera did not ask Ren to stay. She had written one letter. She would not spend a second hope on the same stamp. Illa, behind her, lifted two fingers in a salute she had stolen from some old soldier's funeral and went back into the house before her face could ask him to be a sun that belonged to them.

Ren stood. The village ticked with hidden warmth, a clock that would run down. He had shared heat the way he had shared stolen shard-crumbs in Vireth, and the world had answered by giving him a name he had not wanted and a crown he was not allowed to wear. West and south the tax-road ran toward glass and cities and a Church that had already begun to invent a saint to put in his place. He scratched Cinder's ears, felt Lyra at his shoulder and Orin folding the map, and started walking while Thornveil still believed in him, because belief was a heat too, and he was beginning to understand how quickly it could be tithed.
