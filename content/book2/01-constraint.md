# Chapter 1
## Constraint

Ren ran the ice-hall the way a thief ran a roof: light, ugly, and with one eye on the drop. The drop here was heat. Every stride put a pale print on the nave floor, and the print wanted to be a puddle, and the puddle wanted to be a river that would take the whole ruin down to black water. He kept the Primordial Calor locked behind his teeth and felt it lean anyway, patient as a dog at a butcher's door. The hall had been a church before the Hollow Sun learned to be a disk. Frost had grown in through the broken rose window and made a second architecture of ribs and needles. If he melted it, Lyra would make a third architecture out of his ribs, and he was trying to be teachable.

"Inside," she called from the altar steps, which were not steps so much as a slumped white stair of old ice. "Not the floor. Not the air. You. If I see steam I will put you through a window that still has glass." Orin sat on a fallen pew with a slate on his knees and the look of a boy who had been born correcting people. "Heat is not a pet you keep in your chest. It is a gradient. You are asking a star to pretend it is a candle. Watch the groins of the vault. If they weep, you have already lost."

"Thank you," Ren said, and copied the Glassheart on the way past him because copying was the one trick that did not ask permission. Orin's technique sat in the world like a theorem: heat folded into itself, a lantern with no chimney. Ren wore it for six strides and the floor stopped hissing. On the seventh the copy slipped, because theorems did not like being stolen by a street rat who had learned arithmetic off ration-chalk, and a seam of water opened under his heel with a sound like a slapped mouth. Cinder, sleeping in the lee of a saint's broken feet, sneezed at the warmth as if it had offended her. Three legs, one opinion. Ren hopped, cursed, and shut the leak with a grimace that felt like swallowing a coal.

He started again from the ruined font. The point of the exercise was not power. He had power the way a well had drowning. The point was to move through a place that would die if he breathed wrong, and to leave it as he found it, which was the opposite of every instinct that had kept him alive in Vireth. In the ash-slums you took heat when it showed its throat. You fed Cinder and Toma and Nima and you called that morality because the Church of the Last Ember had already spent the other kind on processions and locked gates. Here the ice-hall was the lesson and the cage. Lyra had chosen it on purpose. She had a talent for choosing rooms that hated him.

Noon at the pass still lived in his wrists. Thirty heartbeats of a sky that remembered gold. The disk of the Hollow Sun had twitched in its black socket as if a sleeper had heard his name, and then the light had gone out of him so hard he had vomited frost-melt and slept with Lyra's seals painted on his collarbones like bruises that meant well. He had woken to Orin arguing with a barometric note and Lyra sharpening a saber on a stone that did not deserve her, and the three of them had walked until the ruin found them: a church that had failed at worship and succeeded at cold. Training, she called it. Constraint. Do not melt the world every time it looks at you hungry.

He got a full circuit without water. The Glassheart held. His breath did not fog, which was a terrible feeling, like being slightly dead. At the far apse he put his palm on a column and felt the ice's slow mind, the way it remembered being rain. He could have poured himself into that memory and made a spring. He did not. He turned, and Lyra's mouth did a small unkind thing that might have been approval if you had known her long enough to collect rare animals. "Again," she said. "Then we eat whatever Orin pretends is stew." Orin did not look up from the slate. "It is stew. It contains water, salt, and the idea of a hare."

Ren ran it again because he was stubborn, and because the Calor liked motion more than stillness, and because if he stood still the Hollow Sun's dream pressed against the back of his eyes like a thumb. He did not want to be lucid today. Lucid meant the disk looking back. He kept the heat in the shape Orin had written, a closed circuit, a private sun the size of a fist, and when he finished the second circuit the vault had not wept and Lyra had not thrown him through glass, which counted as a festival in their little liturgy. They ate in the sacristy, where frost had made lace of old vestments and Cinder was permitted on the table because the table had already lost that argument. The stew tasted of smoke and apology. Outside, the Nightlands creaked, and far west a thin smear of chimney-smoke leaned over a valley and failed to be comforting.

"You're leaking," Lyra said, not looking at him. He told her he was eating. She told him his left hand warmed the bowl when he thought, and that he should stop thinking or stop holding crockery. He set the bowl down. The wood steamed anyway, traitorously fond of him. Orin made a note. After the meal he went out because the ice-hall made his teeth ache with unspent heat, and because Cinder needed a patrol that was not a nave. The valley below the church fell away in terraces of black stone and white moss. A tax-road, old and unloved, cut the lower snow like a scar that had never been paid to heal. He was halfway down the scree, talking nonsense to the dog, when he saw the fox.

It was a miserable geometry of fur and intention, collapsed in a bowl of ice where a creek had died standing up. Its breath was a thread. One foreleg was wrong. In Vireth he had seen children like that, not foxes, and the distinction had never impressed him. The Calor rose without being asked. He told it no, the way Lyra had taught him, the way the ice-hall required. The fox looked at him with a black wet eye and did not understand theology. "Don't," he told himself, which was a formality, and then he put his hands on the frozen creek and let a narrow, shameful kindness out. Ice went to water and water went to a bright little run that found its old bed as if it had been waiting under a door. Grass that had been a rumor stood up green for a strip the width of a street. The fox screamed, which was ungrateful and fair, then dragged itself to the thawed mud and lay there shaking while feeling returned. Cinder sniffed it, found it insufficiently dog, and sat on Ren's foot in case the valley got ideas.

The punch landed high on his shoulder, not his face, which was how he knew she was still trying not to kill him. He went sideways into slush. Lyra stood over him with both sabers sheathed and her inquisitor's coat open to the cold as if cold were a junior officer she refused to acknowledge. The anti-magic seals at her wrists were dark with use, little branded geometries that ate stray heat the way Cinder ate anything unattended. "A fox," she said. "You lit a valley for a fox." He told her it was dying. She said everything was dying, that was the arrangement, and he had just rung a bell the Church could hear from Cindrel. She hauled him up by the collar, which had been a sack before it was a shirt, and shook him once, economical. "Constraint. The word. You want to feed every broken thing you see, you will feed the Hollow Sun a map of your walking, and then you will feed it us."

He could have burned her hand off his collar. The thought arrived and left. He said it had been a creek, a small one, that he had not even used Dominion. She let go. Her voice dropped into the dry register she used when she was furious enough to be precise. He had barely woken grass in a country that had not had grass since her grandmother's grandmother stopped being a story. Solenne did not need a miracle. She needed a rumor. Boy Who Lit the Sky warms a valley for vermin, and every settlement on a tax-well would start praying in the wrong direction. Then the Saint would come. Then Lyra would get to choose which of them she buried. Cinder growled at her boots, loyal and badly informed. Ren rubbed his shoulder and looked at the strip of impossible green, already frosting at the edges. He had not saved a valley. He had postponed a death and signed his name in meltwater.

Orin came down the scree with his coat too fine for the weather and his hair full of ice-hall dust. He did not ask about the punch. He looked at the creek, at the fox, at Ren's hands, and filed it, then held up a scrap of bark with charcoal on it, the sort of letter the outer roads still carried by hungry boys. "Thornveil," Orin said. "Two ridges west. They sit on a Church tax-well. The tithe increased after your noon. They have been paying in degrees. The well is near dry. The letter is from a woman who still believes in post." Ren took the bark. The letters were clumsy and sure. *Children first. Then the old. The well takes and does not give back. If the boy from the pass is real, he should know what his sky cost.* A pressed frost-flower had been stuck to the underside and had shattered in Orin's pack, leaving a pale stain like a small death.

Lyra read it over his shoulder. She did not swear. If they went there he would do exactly what he had done for the fox, and he would do it to a village, and the disk would twitch, and she would be very tired. "They're dying on a well that isn't theirs," Ren said. "That's not a fox. That's Vireth with prettier snow." She flicked the bark and called it a trap with prettier snow. Solenne could write a desperate woman as easily as she could unmake a child. He had seen her handwriting. It looked like mercy.

Orin cleared his throat, which he did when he was about to be useful and hated it. The tax-road was watched. The well itself would have a warden and a null-brand. If Ren shared heat in the open, the brand might rupture the way Vireth's had, and that was not a fox either, that was a second sky. He paused, and the genius in him visibly lost a fight with the brother. "They will freeze by week's end if the letter is honest. Lirae froze in a house with a name. These people will freeze in a ledger." Wind came down the valley and tried to take the green away. Ren thought of Nima's bread and Toma's hands and the Wellgate riot, of heat hoarded behind iron and a cardinal who called scarcity holy. He thought of the oath he had made in Mother Ash's white court: no sun that belonged to anyone. A tax-well was a sun with a lock on it. The Calor turned over, interested.

Lyra watched his face and saw the answer before he said it, because she had hunted him across a city and then defected into his weather. "We go at dusk," she said, already angry at her own mouth. "You do not light the well. You do not light the sky. You do not light anything that has a name on a map. You take what they can hide in a cellar and you leave the rest of the world cold. If you warm another valley for an animal I will throw you into the tax-well myself and tell Solenne she can have the steam."

Ren picked up Cinder's lean weight under one arm because the scree was cruel to three legs, and looked west where Thornveil's smoke had thinned to a thread. The ice-hall above them kept its winter, unmelted, a small ugly victory. Constraint, she called it. He could hold a church. He did not know if he could hold a village and not become a god the Church would have to kill. He started walking anyway, because the letter had a frost-flower on it and because he had never learned to pass a dying thing and call that wisdom.
